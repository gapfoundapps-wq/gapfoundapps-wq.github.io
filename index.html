Gap Found

Software for the problems nobody has built for

Most software competes in categories that already exist. We go looking for the gaps — the things people work around every day, by hand, because no one thought to build the tool.

The idea

There is a long tail of real problems that never get software written for them. Not because they are unimportant — often the opposite — but because they are unglamorous, narrow, heavily regulated, or belong to people who are not the sort anyone builds for.

So the work gets done on paper, in a spreadsheet that one person understands, or in someone's head. It works right up until it does not.

A gap is not an idea for an app. It is a problem someone is already solving badly, at a cost they have stopped noticing.

Gap Found exists to find those and close them. We are less interested in what a market wants more of, and more interested in what a specific person cannot currently do at all.

How we choose what to build

Someone is already paying the cost

In time, in risk, or in fines. If nobody is bearing a cost today, there is no gap — there is just an idea.

The problem has a hard edge

A deadline, an obligation, a number that must not be exceeded. Vague problems make vague software.

It is too specific for anyone else

The categories big enough to attract competitors are rarely the ones that are underserved.

Being wrong would matter

If getting the detail wrong has consequences, that is a reason to build it carefully — not a reason to avoid it.

How we build

Facts are data, not code

Where our software depends on something that changes — a rule, a limit, a threshold — it is stored as a dated record with a citation and a link to the source, not buried in the app as a constant.

A person checks what matters

Changes are detected automatically and confirmed by a human before they reach anyone. We will not silently rewrite a number that someone is judged against.

Honest about scope

We say plainly what our software is and is not, where it applies, and where it does not. Overstating what a tool does is how people get hurt.

Hold the least data that works

Where sensitive information is unavoidable, we keep the minimum that does the job, and say exactly what we do not collect.

FIRST PRODUCT

Airtight

Australia is tightening its rules on crystalline silica — the dust produced when stone, porcelain, concrete, brick and tile are cut, ground, drilled or polished. It causes silicosis, which is irreversible. New workplace exposure limits take effect on 1 December 2026.

The businesses carrying that obligation are mostly small: a stonemason with four staff, a tiler, a concrete cutter. The rules require written risk control plans, air monitoring, worker health monitoring, training records and notifications to a regulator — and no compliance department to keep track of any of it.

That is the gap. Airtight assembles those records, tracks the law as it changes, and tells a business plainly which records it could not produce if an inspector walked in tomorrow.
